Training Zones

Find Your Correct Training Zones

Three variables drive endurance performance. Only one is genetic — the other two you can train. Here's how to find and use them on the water.

December 20, 2023 8 min read By Mick Di Betta

In endurance sports, three variables are of prime importance to performance: VO₂max (largely genetically determined), anaerobic threshold, and economy. The good news: while VO₂max sets a ceiling, the other two respond dramatically to the right training — and those are the ones we can actually prescribe for.

Anaerobic threshold, for our purposes, is equivalent to OBLA — the onset of blood lactate accumulation, or the exercise intensity at which lactate production equals its clearance. Economy is the percentage of VO₂max you can sustain at a given submaximal intensity. Both can be trained. Both are measurable on the water.

Breaking Down VO₂max for Practical Use

VO₂max alone is complicated to measure without expensive equipment. But if we separate it into two trainable variables — vVO₂max (the velocity at VO₂max) and tlimVO₂max (the maximum time you can sustain that velocity) — we have something we can actually work with on the water.

Why this matters: vVO₂max and tlimVO₂max can both be greatly improved through training, even if VO₂max itself is genetically bounded. Improving tlimVO₂max also improves your lactate threshold — so sessions targeting these variables deliver multiple adaptations simultaneously.

Field Testing on the Water

Before testing, a few rules that matter:

  • Always test at the same location when possible
  • Test at the same time of day
  • Keep the same warm-up protocol each time
  • The day before should be an easy session
  • Eat the same foods in the 24 hours prior

Step 1 — Find your vVO₂max

Find a stretch of relatively sheltered water, away from wind and strong current. Complete a 12–15 minute warm-up, paddling up and back on the course you'll use for the test.

Then: start at a speed you're comfortable holding for 2 minutes, and increase by 0.5 km/h every 1 minute until you can no longer hold or increase that speed. The speed at which you have to slow down is your vVO₂max.

Example — Elite prone paddler: Starting at 8 km/h → 8.5 → 9 → 9.5 → 10 → 10.5 → at 10.7 km/h the paddler had to slow down. vVO₂max = 10.7 km/h.

Step 2 — Find your tlimVO₂max

After at least 48 hours of recovery, paddle at your vVO₂max for as long as you can maintain that exact speed. The duration you hold it is your tlimVO₂max.

Example continued: The same elite prone paddler paddling at 10.7 km/h held it for 3 minutes 45 seconds. tlimVO₂max = 3:45.

You now have two measurements — speed and duration — that accurately reflect your current conditioning and can be used to prescribe individualised training sessions.

Using Your Numbers to Prescribe Training

Training sessions targeting vVO₂max have been a cornerstone of serious endurance coaching for years. The key insight — one that took coaches time to discover — is that every athlete should train at their own vVO₂max, not the same pace as the group. GPS tracking makes this precise and individual.

The tlimVO₂max is equally individual and positively correlated to lactate threshold: the higher the LT, the higher the tlimVO₂max. This is why the sessions used at All Paddling can simultaneously improve vVO₂max, tlimVO₂max, LT, and paddling economy in the same block of training.

From these two test numbers, your correct training speeds for all five training zones can be calculated — giving you confidence that every interval session on the water is doing exactly what it's supposed to do.
