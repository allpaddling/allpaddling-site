Mental Performance

Balancing the Physiological and Psychological

Training techniques vary wildly across sports — but examine what elite endurance athletes actually do, and the same patterns emerge. Here's what the mental side of a great training plan looks like.

December 20, 2023 8 min read By Mick Di Betta

Training techniques vary massively across sports and athletes worldwide — but examine the contents and actions of elite athletes during their sessions, and you will find a consistent balance: equal importance placed on the physiological makeup of the session and the psychological approach to how it is performed.

The Physiological Side

In paddling, we use shortened intervals of the complete competition distance, with rest periods designed to mimic the race we're training for. This lets the paddler break the race duration into shorter, intense intervals — performed at speeds above and below racing pace — with varying periods of rest.

This practice allows the paddler to increase their physiological conditioning to that race distance. But it does something equally important: by breaking the race into shorter intervals, it gives the athlete time to psychologically experience the feel of every part of the race — the duration, the discomfort, and the skills required to successfully execute it.

The principle: "The more times you have been there, the easier it will be when you get there." Multiple repeats of race-segment intervals allow many opportunities to physiologically and psychologically experience racing conditions — under less pressure than competition, but enough stress to create genuine adaptation.

The Four Mental Qualities

The psychological content of a well-designed training plan addresses four mental qualities that research consistently identifies as critical to competitive performance:

Concentration

Sustaining attention on selected stimuli. Effective concentration is effortless — it comes naturally when the mind is consumed with the immediate situation.

Confidence

The belief that you can succeed in this moment, at this distance. Fragile under pressure — built through repeated exposure to race-specific conditions.

Control

Managing emotions — especially anxiety and anger — in the face of adversity. The ability to remain positive when things get hard.

Commitment

The resolve to continue. Driven by enjoyment, investment, and social support — and the strongest predictor of long-term sport participation.

Concentration

Concentration is the ability to sustain attention on selected stimuli. It can be disrupted by our own thoughts and feelings. Intense concentration requires emotional energy — and counterintuitively, the harder athletes try to concentrate, the more it can slip away. Effective concentration is effortless, coming naturally when the athlete is absorbed in the competition and paying attention to exactly the right cues.

Confidence

Elite athletes often describe confidence as fragile, especially under pressure. Research identifies two types:

  • Trait confidence — a general belief in your ability to succeed in sport
  • State confidence — the belief that you can succeed right now, in this race, at this distance

A well-structured physiological training plan builds state confidence through skill-specific drills at racing intensities. Race-specific training creates the experiences that make confidence robust.

Control

Two emotions — anxiety and anger — are most consistently associated with poor performance. Emotions have consequences that ripple across performance:

  • Physiological: heightened arousal affects power, muscular tension, and coordination
  • Cognitive: narrows or shifts attention — which can help or hurt depending on whether the focus lands on relevant or irrelevant cues
  • Motivational: dysfunctional emotions result in inappropriate energy distribution; over time they affect persistence and commitment

Commitment

Sport commitment is defined as the psychological state representing the desire to continue sport participation. Among all factors that influence it, enjoyment has consistently proven to be the strongest predictor — particularly for youth athletes. Involvement opportunities and social support follow closely.

Motivation is the psychological energy that initiates and sustains behaviour. Understanding what drives commitment — and structuring training to protect and reinforce it — is as much a coaching responsibility as designing the physiological plan.

Putting It Together

The physiological make-up of your training plan — the specific sessions, volumes, and intensities — should be a structured section of your goal race, repeated progressively over months. A good coach helps you design that structure.

But the psychological content of that same plan is equally non-negotiable. Training that addresses concentration, confidence, control, and commitment doesn't require separate "mental skills sessions" — it's baked into race-specific intervals, progressive loading, and consistent execution over time.
