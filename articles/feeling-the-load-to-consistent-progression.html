Training Metrics

Feeling the Load to Consistent Progression

External load tells you how much work you did. Internal load tells you how hard it actually was. Understanding the relationship between the two is how you get fitter — not overtrained.

December 20, 2023 5 min read By Mick Di Betta

There are two basic kinds of training load. External training load is an objective measure of how much work you did: distance covered, pace, power output. Internal training load represents how hard you — as an individual — had to work to complete that session. It might be measured by average heart rate, or simply by your subjective sense of effort and fatigue.

Both matter. But it's the internal load — the actual level of physiological stress your body experiences — that determines whether you'll get fitter or overtrained.

The Fundamental Question

How hard was that workout you just finished? There are many ways to answer it — heart rate monitors, GPS pace, gut feeling. One way or another, you need a sense of how hard you're working to make good decisions about how to distribute your efforts each session and week, and how to gradually increase your training load over months.

The simplest internal load tool: During and shortly after each interval or session, ask yourself: "How hard was that?" on a scale from 1 to 10. This is the RPE scale — Rate of Perceived Exertion. It's free, immediate, and never runs out of batteries.

Research has shown that a well-calibrated RPE assessment gives as much information about training effort as sophisticated physiological analysis. Your internal computer is as sensitively tuned to the subtleties of effort as any external gadget — the trick is developing the habit of reading it accurately.

Internal and External Together

The message here isn't to ditch your GPS or heart rate monitor — it's that neither internal nor external data is complete on its own. What matters is the relationship between the two:

  • In training, it's meaningless to know your internal load is going up if you don't also know whether you're going faster or slower
  • In racing, it's counterproductive to chase a target pace without considering how you feel that day, or the conditions — currents, wind, tides — you're actually paddling in

So the goal is to trust your feel (RPE) and trust in it — while using external data to validate and contextualise what you're sensing. When internal and external data agree, you're training well. When they diverge significantly, something important is happening: either you're getting fitter (same RPE at higher pace) or accumulating more fatigue than the numbers suggest (same pace at higher RPE).

Developing that awareness — the ability to read the relationship between how hard it feels and what it's actually producing — is what consistent, long-term progression is built on.
