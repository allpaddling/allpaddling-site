Training Metrics

How Do You Rate Your Training Exertion?

Do you really need a heart rate monitor to know how hard you're working? Your own sense of exertion is nearly as accurate as the latest technology — and it never runs out of batteries.

December 20, 2023 7 min read By Mick Di Betta

How we perceive our training regime differs for every athlete. Many paddlers find themselves in periods where motivation dips and quality slides — training continues but the accelerator comes off, and the GPS results reveal the truth. Getting back to basics — monitoring the feel of each stroke against speed on the water — can reignite motivation in a single session.

The question is: do you really need a heart rate monitor to tell you how hard you're working? Research suggests you probably don't. Perceived Exertion is your innate measure of how hard your body is working — encompassing heart rate, respiration, sweating, muscle fatigue, and more. And your sense of it is nearly as accurate as measurable variables like heart rate and lactate threshold.

The RPE Scale

Swedish researcher Gunnar Borg developed the RPE (Rate of Perceived Exertion) scale to give athletes a consistent framework for rating their effort. Using the 1–10 version, combined with a GPS watch for time and speed, gives you everything you need to monitor intensity and measure progression.

Think of it like driving a car. The feeling of your foot on the accelerator tells you roughly how fast you're going — and by checking the speedometer, you can adjust precisely. On the water, your RPE tells you the intensity, and your GPS shows the speed. Together, they close the loop.

RPE in Variable Conditions

One of the most common objections to RPE-based training is current and wind: conditions change the speed you see for a given effort. This is actually an argument for RPE, not against it. Training using RPE in variable conditions develops a finely tuned feel on the water and builds the stamina to adjust to resistance from wind, tide, and current without fatiguing as quickly as athletes who rely solely on a target pace number.

The practical approach: train half your session in one direction, half in the other. This gives an accurate average speed as resistance and assistance even out. RPE for intensity, GPS for speed — track both across every session.

The insight that changes training: Trainers who don't understand the feel of their craft under their own power don't get the full benefits of a training program. The combination of felt exertion (RPE) and visual speed monitoring on a GPS ignites the psychological energy needed for maximum physical adaptation.
