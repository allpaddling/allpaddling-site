Mental Performance

Staying in the Moment During Training and Racing

An athlete's ability to manage emotion under pressure is often the difference between winning and finishing. Training your control level is a skill — here's how to build it.

December 20, 2023 6 min read By Mick Di Betta

An athlete's ability to maintain control of their emotions in the face of adversity — and remain positive while doing it — is essential to winning. Two emotions consistently associated with poor performance are anxiety and anger. Both can claim your concentration and narrow your attentional focus at exactly the wrong moment.

Identifying when you feel a particular emotion and understanding the reason for it is the first step toward gaining emotional control. This is a trainable skill, not a fixed personality trait.

Concentration

Concentration is the ability to sustain attention on selected stimuli. It can be disrupted by our own thoughts and feelings that distract us from the task at hand. And importantly: intense concentration requires emotional energy. The harder athletes try to concentrate, the more it can slip away.

Effective concentration is an effortless process. It comes naturally when the mind is completely consumed by the immediate situation — when the athlete is absorbed in the competition, paying attention to just the right cues (interval intensities, stroke rate, water conditions) to perform well.

Key insight: Concentration is dynamic — it constantly shifts from one point to another. A loss of concentration occurs when attention divides or shifts to something irrelevant. Race-specific training creates the familiarity that keeps attention anchored to what matters.

Confidence

Elite athletes often say that confidence is fragile, especially under pressure. Confidence allows the athlete to focus on essential tasks — and fluctuations can mean the difference between a best and worst performance.

Sport psychology breaks self-confidence into two basic categories:

  • Trait confidence — the general belief in your ability to succeed across situations
  • State confidence — the belief that you can succeed in this particular moment, in this race, at this distance

A well-structured training plan builds both, but state confidence is developed specifically through skill-specific drills at racing intensities. The more times you've been in a situation, the more confident you become that you can handle it.

Building Race-Specific Mental Strength

If you want to win — or simply perform at your personal best consistently — the approach is clear: use training programs that strengthen concentration and build confidence through repeated exposure to racing conditions.

The method is race-specific interval training: breaking the race into shortened intervals and completing repetitions at various intensities. This creates maximum adaptation both physiologically and psychologically.

By using multiple repeats of race-segment intervals with controlled rest periods, the athlete experiences racing conditions many times over — under less pressure than competition, but at enough stress to create genuine adaptation. The result is that on race day, the body and mind have already been there. The situation is familiar. The emotions are manageable.

More simply: the more times you have been in that situation, the more you can physically and psychologically cope with it.
