Training Philosophy

Mastering Training to Become a Successful Master Paddler

The 50–65 age group at endurance races includes some of the original legends of the sport — still competitive with paddlers half their age. Here's how to train intelligently as you get older.

December 20, 2023 8 min read By Mick Di Betta

If you follow ocean paddling — OC1, outriggers, traditional prone, ocean ski, or SUP — you've noticed that the average age at endurance races is approximately 40–45 years old. And the 50s and 60s age groups include some of the original legends of the sport, still very competitive with paddlers half their age. How do these 50–65 year-olds have the stamina to finish a 32-mile race and make it to the presentation dinner with a smile and a cold drink?

What Changes With Age

Physical changes to expect include:

  • Loss of muscle size and strength
  • Reduced power output
  • Lower maximum aerobic capacity
  • Increased body fat

Muscle mass starts to decline in the mid-to-late thirties at roughly 3–5% per decade, accelerating to 7–8% per decade in the mid-to-late fifties. This mainly affects fast-twitch fibres — which is why we lose power and speed rather than endurance as we age.

Lift Heavy

Sadly, no amount of paddling, swimming, or running will preserve fast-twitch fibres — endurance activities don't provide sufficient load for that type of adaptation. Instead, lift heavy weights as you age to preserve muscle mass: deadlifts, squats, leg press, bent-over rows — heavy load, low repetitions.

Many aging athletes worry about injury from heavy lifting, and poor technique does create that risk. But with good technique, heavy resistance training delivers multiple benefits: maximal strength improvement, power gains, improved resilience in connective tissues, and in some cases new muscle mass.

Maintain Functional Range of Motion

With age, joints tighten and functional range of movement reduces. For paddling this directly impacts stroke length — and since velocity equals limb frequency multiplied by stroke length, losing range of motion slows you down. Every trainer should aim to avoid injury, but it's even more critical with age, as lost fitness is harder to regain.

Aim for at least 15 minutes of mobility work for every 60 minutes of training each week.

Keep Up the Intensity

As you age, long slow distance should become less of a priority, not more. High-intensity interval training must make up the majority of your training volume. This can seem counter-intuitive, but it works — and if you're healthy and injury-free, you have nothing to fear from it.

Target: Plan regular high-intensity interval workouts and aim to work at an RPE of 6–10 for around 40–50% of total weekly volume. Work at the same percentage above FTP that a younger athlete might. Quality is the key — take longer recovery intervals between repetitions if needed, but maintain the intensity during the work periods.

Listen to Your Body

You've gained athletic wisdom over the years — use it. Recovery between workouts requires adjustment with age. What you could absorb in 48 hours in your thirties may need 72 hours in your sixties. Be more compassionate to your body. You've been using it hard for decades and it has served you well.

As an older, wiser athlete you've hopefully learned to manage your ego — and that's a genuine competitive advantage. Consistent, intelligent training over the long term beats ego-driven overtraining every time. By lifting heavy, maintaining intensity, and looking after your mobility and recovery, you can be healthy and performing well into your later years.
